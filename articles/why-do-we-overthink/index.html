Why Do We Overthink? The Psychology of Repetitive Thinking

Thoughts can repeat when the mind is trying to solve a problem, prepare for uncertainty or revisit something emotionally important. But repeated thinking is not always productive.

Home / Psychology / Why Do We Overthink?
Educational article • Published September 19, 2026 • Research-informed
This article is educational. “Overthinking” is a common everyday term, not a diagnosis. Persistent distress or significant impairment may warrant discussion with an appropriately qualified professional.

The short answer

“Overthinking” often describes repetitive thinking that feels difficult to stop. Research commonly distinguishes forms such as worry and rumination, while the broader term repetitive negative thinking can include both.

Why does the mind keep returning to the same thought?

One reason is that unresolved uncertainty or emotionally important events can keep attention focused on the same topic. Worry tends to involve possible future problems, while rumination often involves repeatedly revisiting negative experiences or their meaning. These processes can overlap rather than fitting into perfectly separate boxes.

When thinking becomes a loop

  • The same question is examined repeatedly without new information.
  • You keep replaying a past conversation but cannot identify a useful next step.
  • You imagine many possible negative outcomes without moving toward a decision.
  • The thinking consumes attention while leaving the underlying problem unchanged.

Research treats repetitive negative thinking as a broader process rather than a single diagnosis. Research studies have found associations between repetitive negative thinking and anxiety or depression symptoms, but these associations do not by themselves show that repetitive thinking alone causes a mental-health condition.

What research does not prove

Not every repeated thought is harmful. Planning, reflection and problem-solving can be useful. The important distinction is whether thinking produces new understanding or action, or simply repeats the same material. Research on repetitive negative thinking is still developing, and different studies use somewhat different definitions and measures.

What can you try in everyday life?

  1. Name the process: Instead of “I must solve this right now,” notice “my mind is repeatedly returning to this problem.”
  2. Separate solvable from unsolvable: If there is a concrete next step, write it down. If there is no new information available, continuing to analyse may not add much.
  3. Set a small decision boundary: Decide what information you actually need and when you will review the issue again.
  4. Return attention to the present: Shift into a concrete activity rather than treating every thought as a problem requiring an immediate answer.
  5. Seek support when needed: If repetitive thinking is persistent, highly distressing or interfering with daily functioning, consider speaking with a qualified professional.

What does treatment research suggest?

A 2025 meta-analysis of 55 studies involving 4,970 participants found a moderate overall post-treatment effect of cognitive behavioural therapy interventions on repetitive negative thinking compared with control groups. It also found larger effects for interventions specifically targeting repetitive negative thinking. This is evidence about treatments studied in research—not a guarantee that one approach will work for every person.

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

Ask: “Is this thought helping me solve something, or am I repeating it?”

If there is a useful next step, take it. If there is no new information or action available, gently redirecting attention may be more useful than continuing the same mental loop.
