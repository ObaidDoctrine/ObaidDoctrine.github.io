What Is Emotional Intelligence?

Emotional intelligence is commonly discussed as a set of abilities and skills involving emotions, perception and interpersonal functioning. The concept has several models, so claims should be made carefully.

This article is educational. Emotional intelligence (EI) is not a single universally defined personality trait, and an online description cannot determine how emotionally intelligent a particular person is.

The short answer

Emotional intelligence refers to ways of understanding and working with emotional information. In one major ability model, EI includes perceiving emotions, using emotional information to support thinking, understanding emotions, and managing emotions. Other models combine emotional abilities with self-reported traits or broader interpersonal characteristics, so the term does not have only one measurement system. citeturn0search12turn0search13

Why there is more than one definition

EI became widely known through both psychological research and popular writing. Scientific work has developed several models and assessment methods, including ability-based measures and self-report or mixed models. These approaches should not be treated as interchangeable because they can measure different constructs. citeturn0search12turn0search13

The four abilities in an influential model

  1. Perceiving emotions: noticing and identifying emotional information in yourself and in other people.
  2. Using emotions: considering emotional information when directing attention, thinking or solving problems.
  3. Understanding emotions: recognizing how emotions can change, combine and relate to situations.
  4. Managing emotions: regulating emotional responses in ways that support goals and effective functioning.

These abilities are not the same thing as “always staying calm.” Emotional intelligence can involve accurately recognizing an uncomfortable emotion and responding to it deliberately rather than simply suppressing it.

What emotional intelligence can look like in everyday life

  • Noticing that frustration is changing the way you interpret a conversation.
  • Checking what another person actually said instead of assuming you know their intention.
  • Putting a name to an emotion before deciding what to do about it.
  • Choosing a response that fits the situation rather than reacting automatically.
  • Recognizing that another person's emotion can be real without automatically accepting every interpretation they make.

APA describes emotions as complex reactions involving experiential, behavioural and physiological elements. That complexity is one reason emotional skills cannot be reduced to a simple “good emotions versus bad emotions” rule. citeturn0search4

EI is not the same as IQ, personality, empathy, or being nice

These concepts overlap in some research but are not identical. Ability-based EI is specifically concerned with processing emotional information, while some broader EI models include traits or self-perceptions. Research reviews therefore recommend being precise about which model and measure is being discussed. citeturn0search13

How to practice emotional skills without turning them into labels

  1. Pause: notice the emotion before acting on it.
  2. Name: use a specific word such as disappointment, worry, irritation, embarrassment or relief.
  3. Check the trigger: ask what happened and what meaning you assigned to it.
  4. Check the evidence: separate what you know from what you are assuming about another person's thoughts.
  5. Choose the response: consider what action fits your goal and the situation.
  6. Review: after the interaction, ask what worked and what you would change next time.

What research can tell us—and what it cannot

A large meta-analysis of emotional intelligence and academic performance combined 158 studies involving more than 42,000 students and found associations between EI and academic outcomes. The authors also examined different EI conceptualizations and measures, illustrating why the exact definition and assessment matter. citeturn0search13

That evidence does not mean EI is a single skill that guarantees success, or that a score can explain someone's whole personality or future. Different measures can capture different aspects of emotional functioning, and relationships between variables do not automatically establish causation.

Why self-awareness matters

One useful starting point is noticing the difference between emotion, interpretation, and action. “I feel rejected” is an emotional experience; “they deliberately ignored me” is an interpretation; sending an angry message is an action. Separating these steps creates more room for deliberate choices.

The key idea

Emotional intelligence is better understood as a research area with multiple models than as a fashionable label for people who are “good with emotions.” The practical goal is not to eliminate difficult emotions. It is to become more accurate about emotional information and more deliberate about how you respond to it.
