The short answer
A good psychology claim should survive basic checks: What exactly is being claimed? What evidence supports it? Who was studied? How was the study designed? How large was the effect? What limitations were reported? And does the headline say more than the research actually shows?
Start with the exact claim
Online headlines often compress a complicated study into one memorable sentence. Rewrite the claim in precise terms before judging it. “This study found an association between X and Y in this sample” is a different claim from “X causes Y in everyone.”
Find the original evidence
A social-media post, news article or influencer video may be useful for discovering a study, but it is not the same thing as examining the evidence. Whenever possible, find the original paper, systematic review or authoritative research summary. APA's evidence-based practice policy emphasizes using the best available research while considering the broader body of evidence rather than treating one finding as the whole answer.
Check who was actually studied
Look at the sample: age, location, number of participants, relevant characteristics and how participants were recruited. A result from a specific group may not automatically apply to every population. This is especially important in psychology because behaviour can vary with development, culture, context and measurement.
Check the study design
Correlation: shows that variables are related; by itself it does not establish that one caused the other.
Experiment: can provide stronger evidence about causal effects when the design and implementation support causal inference.
Longitudinal study: follows participants over time and can help examine temporal relationships, but does not automatically prove causation.
Systematic review or meta-analysis: combines evidence across studies using defined methods; its quality depends on the studies and methods included.
Look beyond “statistically significant”
A statistically significant result does not automatically mean an effect is large, important or useful in everyday life. Consider the size of the effect, uncertainty around the estimate, the outcome being measured and whether the result has practical importance.
Read the limitations
Limitations are not a formality. They tell you where a study's conclusions should be narrowed. Common issues include small or selective samples, self-report measures, short follow-up, uncontrolled confounding, measurement differences and difficulty generalizing beyond the study population.
Watch for common online warning signs
“Scientists prove…” based on one small study.
A correlation described as proof of cause.
A study on animals, a laboratory task or a narrow sample presented as a universal fact about people.
A dramatic percentage with no explanation of what the percentage measures.
A headline that hides uncertainty or important qualifications.
A source that provides no study, author, journal or evidence trail.
Repeated claims presented as true simply because they appear many times.
APA's misinformation research notes that repeated information can become more believable, and that people often focus on understanding information rather than stopping to evaluate its accuracy. That makes deliberate checking especially useful online.
Do not confuse confidence with evidence
A polished website, confident speaker, large follower count or professional-looking graphic does not establish that a claim is scientifically supported. Source quality depends on the evidence and how transparently it is presented.
A 60-second psychology claim check
Claim: What exactly is being said?
Source: Who is making the claim, and can you trace it to evidence?
Study: What research was actually done?
Sample: Who participated?
Design: What can this design reasonably show?
Effect: How large and meaningful was the finding?
Limits: What did the researchers say could not be concluded?
Cross-check: Do other credible studies or reviews point in the same direction?
What to do when the evidence is mixed
“Mixed evidence” is not the same as “nothing is known.” It means confidence should reflect the pattern and quality of available findings. APA's misinformation guidance also emphasizes that many psychological effects are modest and that laboratory findings may not fully predict real-world outcomes.
Example: a viral psychology headline
Suppose a post says, “Researchers discovered that people who do X become more confident.” Before accepting it, ask: Was the study experimental or correlational? How was confidence measured? Who participated? How large was the effect? Was the finding replicated? Did the researchers actually use the word “become,” or did the headline turn an association into a causal claim?
The key idea
Scientific literacy does not mean rejecting psychology claims. It means slowing down enough to understand what the evidence can support, what it cannot support, and how confident you should be. The strongest online habit is not “believe” or “disbelieve” first—it is check first .
