The short answer
“Overthinking” is an everyday term for thinking that feels repetitive, difficult to disengage from, or unproductive. Psychology research more often uses specific concepts such as worry , rumination , and the broader process of repetitive negative thinking (RNT) . These concepts overlap, but they are not identical.
Worry, rumination and repetitive negative thinking
Worry commonly focuses on possible future problems and uncertainty.
Rumination often repeatedly revisits negative experiences, feelings, causes or meanings.
RNT is a broader research construct that can include recurring negative thought processes across different problems.
Calling all repeated thinking “overthinking” can hide these differences. The useful question is not simply how much someone thinks, but what the thinking is doing and whether it leads to new information, a decision, understanding or action.
Why can the mind get stuck?
Repeated thinking can feel like problem-solving because the mind is trying to reduce uncertainty, make sense of an experience or regain a sense of control. Research describes RNT as recurring mental activity that can be difficult to disengage from, while emphasizing that worry and rumination are broad terms rather than single mechanisms.
Context also matters. Uncertainty, emotionally important events, stress, self-criticism and difficult decisions can all make a thought easier to return to. This does not mean that everyone who overthinks has a mental disorder.
When thinking becomes a loop
The same question is examined repeatedly without meaningful new information.
You replay a past conversation but do not move toward a useful next step.
You generate many possible negative outcomes without deciding what information is actually needed.
The thinking takes attention away from sleep, study, work, relationships or ordinary activities.
RNT is associated with anxiety and depression across research, but an association does not prove that repetitive thinking alone causes a particular condition. “Overthinking” itself is not a diagnosis.
Thinking that helps vs. thinking that loops
What can you try in everyday life?
Name the process: Notice “I am looping on this thought” rather than treating every thought as a problem that must be solved immediately.
Define the question: Write the exact decision or uncertainty in one sentence.
Separate solvable from unsolvable: If there is a concrete action or missing information, identify it. If there is nothing useful to add right now, pause the analysis.
Set a review point: Decide when you will return to the issue instead of checking it continuously.
Shift from analysis to action: Take one small, relevant step when action is possible; otherwise deliberately return attention to an ordinary activity.
Reassess the pattern: If the loop is persistent, highly distressing or interfering with daily functioning, talk with a qualified professional.
What does treatment research suggest?
A 2025 transdiagnostic meta-analysis included 55 studies with 4,970 participants and found a moderate overall post-treatment effect of CBT interventions on RNT compared with control groups. Interventions specifically targeting RNT showed larger effects than less specific approaches in that analysis. These are group-level research findings, not a guarantee that a particular treatment will work for every person.
Earlier meta-analysis research also found reductions in RNT following psychological treatments for depression, while emphasizing the need for further research on how changes in RNT relate to later outcomes.
What the evidence does—and does not—tell us
Repeated negative thinking is a meaningful research process, not simply a character flaw.
Research supports links between RNT and several forms of psychological distress, but links are not proof of one-way causation.
Different studies define and measure RNT somewhat differently.
Treatment evidence comes from specific populations and interventions; it should not be turned into a promise for every individual.
When extra support may help
If repetitive thinking is persistent, causes substantial distress, disrupts sleep, study, work or relationships, or feels difficult to manage, discussing the pattern with a qualified mental-health professional can be appropriate. Seeking help does not require self-diagnosing a disorder.
PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY
Ask: “Is this thought helping me solve something, or am I repeating it?”
If there is a useful next step, take it. If there is no new information or action available, gently redirecting attention may be more useful than continuing the same mental loop.
