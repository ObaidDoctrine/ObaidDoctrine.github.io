The short answer
In psychology, forgiveness generally refers to a change in how a person responds to someone who has caused harm—such as a reduction in resentment or movement toward a less hostile response. The APA distinguishes forgiveness from reconciliation and from excusing the harmful act.
Forgiveness is not the same as reconciliation
Reconciliation involves rebuilding or continuing a relationship; forgiveness can occur without returning to the same level of contact. This distinction matters because someone can work through resentment while still keeping boundaries or deciding that a relationship should not continue in its previous form.
Why forgiveness can be psychologically complicated
When someone close behaves badly, people may experience competing concerns: protecting their values, responding to the harm, and preserving an important relationship. Research summarized by the APA found that participants judged close others less harshly than strangers after moral violations, illustrating how relationship closeness can affect responses to wrongdoing.
Forgiveness can also involve anger, grief, disappointment and questions about trust. It is therefore better understood as a process than as a single decision that someone must make immediately.
What forgiveness does not require
It does not require pretending the harm was acceptable.
It does not require forgetting the event or abandoning reasonable boundaries.
It does not require trusting the person again before trust has been rebuilt.
It does not require reconciliation or continued contact.
It does not mean there should be no accountability for harmful behaviour.
A practical way to think about forgiveness
Name what happened: Separate the actual behaviour from assumptions about motives.
Recognize the impact: Notice the emotions, losses, disappointments or changes in trust that followed.
Decide what you need now: That may include an apology, accountability, distance, a boundary, or time.
Separate forgiveness from access: Letting go of some resentment does not automatically mean giving someone the same access to you.
Allow the process to take time: Forgiveness is not a test of how quickly a person can stop feeling hurt.
Where apologies and repair fit
An apology can be one part of relationship repair, especially when it communicates concern and a willingness to make amends. APA's coverage of apology research notes that apologies can help repair relationships, but the quality and sincerity of the apology matter. Forgiveness, however, remains the choice of the person who was hurt; an apology does not create an automatic obligation to forgive or reconcile.
What research does not automatically prove
Research on forgiveness includes different measures, populations and interventions. Evidence that forgiveness is associated with better well-being does not mean that every person should forgive every harm, or that forgiveness alone will repair a relationship. APA materials also describe forgiveness as one possible coping response rather than equating it with reconciliation.
When forgiveness should not replace safety or accountability
If a relationship involves ongoing intimidation, coercion, threats or other serious harm, the priority is safety and appropriate support—not pressure to forgive quickly. Forgiveness should never be presented as a reason to tolerate continuing harm.
The key idea
Forgiveness can be meaningful, but it is neither a shortcut nor a duty. A healthier framework is to separate four questions: What happened? What impact did it have? What boundaries or accountability are needed? and What, if anything, do I want to do with the relationship now?
