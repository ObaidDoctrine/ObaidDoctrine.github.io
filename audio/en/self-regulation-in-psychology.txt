The short answer
In psychology, self-regulation refers broadly to processes through which people monitor and adjust their thoughts, emotions, attention or behaviour in relation to goals, standards or changing circumstances. It can involve starting, stopping, continuing or changing a response. A useful summary is Goal → Monitor → Evaluate → Adjust → Continue .
How does self-regulation work?
Goal or standard: identify what outcome or behaviour matters.
Monitor: notice what you are thinking, feeling or doing.
Evaluate: compare the current state with the goal or standard.
Adjust: change the response, strategy, environment or next step when needed.
Continue or revise: persist when the strategy is working, or revise the plan when evidence shows it is not.
This is an explanatory model, not a claim that every act of self-regulation follows the same conscious sequence.
What can people regulate?
Self-regulation can involve several domains. People may regulate behaviour , such as following a plan; attention , such as returning to a task after distraction; emotion , such as changing how they respond to anger; and thoughts or strategies , such as reconsidering an unhelpful interpretation. The exact construct depends on the research tradition and measurement used.
Self-regulation and long-term goals
Long-term goals often compete with immediate rewards, discomfort or distractions. Self-regulation helps explain how people can keep a longer-term standard in mind while responding to what is happening now. But it is not simply a battle between a “strong” and “weak” self. The environment, task design, available resources and competing goals can change how difficult regulation becomes.
For example, someone who wants to study for an hour may notice repeated phone checking. Regulation could involve returning attention to the task, but it could also involve changing the environment—placing the phone elsewhere, reducing notifications or defining a smaller next step. The second approach matters because self-regulation can include changing conditions rather than relying only on internal effort.
Self-regulation vs self-control
Self-regulation is broader than self-control. Self-control often refers to managing an immediate impulse or response when it conflicts with a longer-term goal. Self-regulation can include that process, but it also includes monitoring, planning, evaluating progress and changing strategies.
So if someone wants to avoid checking their phone during study, resisting the impulse is one self-control moment. Deciding when and where to study, monitoring distraction, changing the study environment and reviewing whether the plan worked are broader self-regulatory processes.
Self-regulation vs motivation, willpower and discipline
Motivation concerns processes that activate and direct behaviour toward valued outcomes, needs or goals. Self-regulation concerns monitoring and adjustment while pursuing those outcomes. A person can strongly value a goal and still need regulation strategies to act consistently.
Willpower is a common everyday term rather than a complete scientific explanation of self-regulation. Discipline is similarly broad in everyday language. Neither term should be used as a substitute for examining the actual behaviour, context and regulatory process involved.
How habits interact with self-regulation
Habits can provide relatively automatic responses in familiar contexts, while self-regulation can help people maintain or change behaviour when circumstances or goals shift. This is why changing a habit may require more than deciding once to behave differently.

Why can self-regulation become difficult?
Regulation becomes more complicated when goals compete, the desired behaviour is costly, cues strongly trigger an established response, emotional demands are high, or the environment repeatedly rewards another option. Individual differences also matter, but a person's behaviour in one situation should not automatically be treated as a fixed trait.
Research on self-regulation therefore benefits from specifying which behaviour, which goal, which context and which outcome are being studied. Broad statements such as “people have a fixed amount of self-control” can hide important differences in measurement and circumstance.
Self-regulation and procrastination
Procrastination is a delay in intended action, but its causes are not reducible to “poor self-control.” Task aversiveness, emotion, temporal discounting, motivation, attention, self-efficacy and context can interact. Self-regulation is relevant because a person must translate an intention into action and adjust when the plan breaks down.

What does research actually tell us?
Self-regulation is a broad research area rather than one single mechanism. Different traditions study goal pursuit, executive control, emotion regulation, self-control and self-regulated learning, sometimes using different definitions and measures. The safest interpretation is therefore to match each claim to the construct and population actually studied.
Academic references describe self-regulation in terms of monitoring, evaluating, inhibiting, persevering and adapting behaviour, attention, emotions or cognitive strategies toward goals. ScienceDirect Topics: Self-Regulation
In educational psychology, self-regulated learning is a related but more specific construct involving learners' active management of cognition, motivation, behaviour and learning conditions. APA Dictionary: self-regulated learning
A practical way to examine self-regulation
Name the goal: What exactly am I trying to do?
Identify the cue: When does the unwanted or desired behaviour usually occur?
Monitor: What happens immediately before and during the behaviour?
Evaluate: What is helping or interfering with the goal?
Adjust: Can I change the next step, strategy or environment?
Review: Did the adjustment work, and what should change next time?
These questions are a behavioural reflection tool, not a diagnostic test or guarantee of behaviour change.
